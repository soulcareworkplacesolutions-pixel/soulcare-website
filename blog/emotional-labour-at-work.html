Emotional Labour at Work: If Empathy Is Expected, Self-Care Must Be Supported

By (Daisy) Tarundeep Kaur, Head, Wellness Operations, Growth and Client Success, SoulCare™ · 25 November 2026

When we hear the term emotional labour, we often think of caregiving or personal relationships. But emotional labour exists in every workplace.

Managers deliver difficult feedback while staying encouraging. HR professionals support employees through crises while managing their own emotions. Customer-facing teams stay calm under pressure, call after call. Colleagues navigate disagreements, reassure anxious teammates and stay professional through their own personal challenges.

This emotional regulation is work. And like any work, it uses up energy.

What emotional labour looks like in an Indian office

  • A team lead who absorbs pressure from above and keeps the team calm below.
  • An HR manager handling a layoff conversation in the morning and an engagement event in the afternoon.
  • A sales or support professional who must sound warm and patient on every call, whatever kind of day they are having.
  • The colleague everyone goes to with their problems, who rarely has anyone to go to with their own.

None of this appears in a job description, yet it shapes how tired people feel at the end of the day far more than the hours they log.

What it costs

High emotional labour, without enough recovery, leads to emotional exhaustion, lower engagement, decision fatigue and falling job satisfaction. Over time it shows up as reduced productivity and higher turnover. When people are constantly managing emotions, their mental energy is pulled away from creativity, collaboration and problem-solving.

The signs are already visible. In Gallup's State of the Global Workplace 2026 report, 31% of employees in India said they had felt anger a lot of the previous day, and 36% sadness. Those feelings do not stay at home. They come to work, and they have to be managed there.

Early signs of emotional exhaustion

  • Feeling flat or numb after conversations that used to feel easy.
  • Irritation over small things, followed by guilt.
  • Avoiding calls, meetings or people who need emotional effort.
  • Needing the whole weekend just to feel normal again.
  • Difficulty switching off, even when the work is done.

Left unaddressed, these can develop into the more visible signs of burnout.

Why "lead with empathy" is not enough

Telling leaders to lead with empathy is good advice, but it is incomplete. Empathy is a skill, and it is also emotional labour. Asking people to give more of it without helping them replenish it is asking them to run on an empty tank.

Sustainable self-care is not an occasional wellness day, an annual retreat or a discount voucher. It is helping people build small daily practices that regulate stress, strengthen emotional resilience and create healthier ways of working.

What organisations can do

  1. Name it. Acknowledge in team conversations that emotional effort is real work. Simply naming it reduces the sense of carrying it alone.
  2. Build recovery into the day. A short pause between difficult calls or meetings, or a ten-minute walk after a tough conversation, helps the nervous system reset.
  3. Rotate the heaviest emotional work. Do not let the same person handle every difficult client or every upset employee.
  4. Support the supporters. Managers and HR teams carry other people's stress. Give them their own space to debrief, with a peer or a professional.
  5. Teach practical tools. Breathing techniques, journaling, reflective art and short movement breaks are simple, and they work when practised regularly.
  6. Measure how people are doing. An anonymous check on stress and energy, repeated over time, shows where support is needed before people burn out.
A question for every manager: who on your team absorbs the most emotional pressure, and when did someone last ask how they are doing?

Investing in what sustains people

The organisations that thrive will not simply expect emotional intelligence. They will invest in the systems that sustain it. Because when people are emotionally supported, they do not just feel better. They lead better, collaborate better and perform better.
