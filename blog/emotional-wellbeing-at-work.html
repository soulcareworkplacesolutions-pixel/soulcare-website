Emotional Wellbeing at Work: How to Build Emotional Balance in Your Team

SoulCare™ Specialist Team · 25 November 2026

Ask most teams how they are, and the answer is "fine". The numbers tell a different story. In Gallup's State of the Global Workplace 2026 report, 36% of employees in India said they had felt sadness a lot of the previous day, 31% anger and 28% loneliness. Only 17% were thriving in their overall lives. Emotional wellbeing at work is not a soft extra. It shapes how people think, decide and treat each other every day.

What emotional balance at work actually means

Emotional balance does not mean staying calm all the time or hiding how you feel. Pressure, frustration and worry are a normal part of any job. Emotional balance is the ability to notice those feelings, respond to them rather than react, and recover afterwards. People with good emotional balance still have hard days. They just do not carry every hard day into the next one.

That recovery is what people mean by emotional resilience, and it can be learned and practised, by individuals and by whole teams.

What knocks teams off balance

In Naukri's 2026 survey of around 10,000 jobseekers, poor work-life balance was the biggest workplace factor affecting mental health, named by 42%. Lack of support from managers followed at 27%, and heavy workload and deadlines at 20%.

Silence makes it worse. In the same survey, 33% of people feared losing career opportunities if they spoke openly about their mental health, and 32% worried that nobody would care.

Signs a team is losing emotional balance

  • Small disagreements turn into sharp exchanges.
  • People stop speaking up in meetings or sharing ideas.
  • Messages arrive late at night and early in the morning.
  • Mistakes rise on routine work.
  • Leave is taken at short notice, often described as "not feeling well".

These often appear long before the more visible signs of burnout.

Seven habits that build emotional balance

  1. Name the feeling. Saying "I am frustrated" or "I am anxious about this deadline", even silently, takes some of its power away.
  2. Pause before you respond. A few slow breaths before replying to a difficult email or comment prevents most regrets.
  3. Set clear edges to the workday. A fixed time to stop checking messages gives the mind a chance to switch off.
  4. Take real breaks. Ten minutes away from the screen, ideally moving or outdoors, resets attention and mood.
  5. Protect sleep. Poor sleep makes every emotion louder the next day.
  6. Stay connected. A short, honest conversation with a colleague or friend is one of the strongest buffers against stress and loneliness.
  7. Move your body. Regular physical activity lifts mood and helps the body clear the tension stress leaves behind.

What managers can do

When asked what would help most, 53% of the people in Naukri's survey chose clearer boundaries between work and personal time, and 22% wanted managers trained to spot burnout. Both are within a manager's reach:

  • Agree on when messages can wait until the next working day, and model it yourself.
  • Open one-to-one meetings with "How are you finding the workload?" before moving to tasks.
  • Make it normal to take a day for mental health, without needing to explain it.
  • Watch for the signs above, and ask privately rather than assume.
  • Plan demanding work for the morning, when people have the most energy. We explain why in Decision Fatigue at Work.
A simple team exercise: at the start of your next team meeting, ask everyone to rate their week from 1 to 5 in a single word or number. No discussion needed. Over a month, the pattern tells you more than any survey.
