Decision Fatigue at Work: Why Your Best People Are Tired by 3 PM

SoulCare™ Specialist Team · 12 November 2026

Your best people are often the most tired by mid-afternoon. They are not lazy, and they are not always overworked in hours. They have simply made hundreds of small decisions since morning: which email first, which client to call back, which problem to escalate, what to say in the next meeting. Every one of those choices draws on the same limited supply of mental energy. That drain is what people call decision fatigue.

What decision fatigue looks like at work

It rarely announces itself. Instead you see small changes in behaviour as the day goes on:

  • Decisions get postponed with "let us discuss tomorrow".
  • People default to the safest option, even when a better one is available.
  • Small requests feel heavier than they should.
  • Irritability rises in late meetings.
  • Focus at work drops, and simple tasks take twice as long.

Managers and team leads are especially exposed, because their role is a long chain of decisions made on behalf of others.

Six ways to reduce decision fatigue

  1. Protect the morning for important decisions. Move reviews, approvals and planning to the first half of the day, when people think most clearly.
  2. Turn repeated choices into simple rules. A standard meeting format, a fixed weekly reporting day or a default template removes dozens of small decisions.
  3. Batch similar tasks. Answer emails in two or three set windows instead of all day. Switching between tasks costs more energy than the tasks themselves.
  4. Cut meeting volume. Ask of every recurring meeting: does this need a decision, or only an update that could be written?
  5. Build real breaks into the day. A ten-minute walk away from the screen restores more focus than another coffee at the desk.
  6. Clear mental clutter at the end of the day. Writing tomorrow's top three priorities before logging off stops unfinished decisions from following people home.

Why this matters for the business

Decision fatigue does not show up on a timesheet, but it shows up in slower approvals, cautious choices and avoidable mistakes late in the day. Reducing it is one of the simplest ways to protect the quality of work from people you already rely on most.

A simple test for your team: ask each person to note the time of day they made their best decision this week. If most answers are before lunch, your calendar is working against your people.
