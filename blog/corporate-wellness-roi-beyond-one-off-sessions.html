Beyond the Checkbox: Rethinking Corporate Wellness ROI

By (Daisy) Tarundeep Kaur, Head, Wellness Operations, Growth and Client Success, SoulCare™ · 25 November 2026

There is a discomfort in genuine change that we often underestimate. When someone has spent months or years working under constant stress, calm can feel unfamiliar. A pause can feel uncomfortable. Slowing down can bring guilt. And when new habits do not feel natural straight away, it is very easy to slip back into the patterns we already know.

That is why I believe wellbeing cannot be created through one-off sessions alone. And it is why so many companies, when they ask about the return on their wellness spend, struggle to find one.

The problem is real, and support is thin

Gallup's State of the Global Workplace 2026 report found that only 17% of employees in India are thriving in their overall lives, and 28% feel stress for much of the day. Access to help is limited too. Mpower's Mental Health and Wellness Quotient survey of 3,000 corporate employees found that only one in ten had access to an Employee Assistance Programme, and stigma still keeps many people from using the support that exists.

So the need is not in doubt. The question HR leaders keep asking me is a fair one: we have run sessions before, so why did nothing change?

Awareness is not transformation

A workshop can introduce mindfulness. A webinar can explain stress. A wellness day can create a welcome moment of relief. All of these have value. But they mostly build awareness, and awareness on its own rarely changes behaviour.

The evidence backs this up. Two of the largest studies of workplace wellness, the Illinois Workplace Wellness Study and a trial across 160 BJ's Wholesale Club stores published in JAMA in 2019, found that the programmes they tested made little measurable difference to health, sick days or productivity. When you look at why, the pattern is familiar: online modules few people finished, one-off events, low participation and no proper before-and-after measurement.

In other words, the failure was usually in the format, not in the people.

What sustainable wellbeing actually needs

In my experience, lasting change needs five things working together:

  • Practice. People need to try a new habit, not just hear about it, and repeat it until it feels normal.
  • Reinforcement. A follow-up session, a reminder or a small group check-in keeps the habit alive past the first busy week.
  • Reflection. Space to notice what is working and what is not, so people can adapt the practice to their own lives.
  • Manager support. If a manager sends messages at midnight, a session on switching off will not survive the week. Leaders have to make the healthier choice possible.
  • Systems. Workloads, meeting habits and policies that make healthier ways of working the easy option, not the brave one.

This is why I design my engagements as journeys rather than single sessions. People learn something, practise it, reflect on it, adapt it, and slowly make healthier choices part of everyday life.

How to measure the return properly

If you want to know whether a wellness programme worked, you have to measure before you start. Without a baseline, any result is a guess. A practical approach for HR teams:

  1. Take an anonymous baseline of how your team is doing on stress, energy, sleep and pain before the first session.
  2. Run the programme over several weeks, not a single afternoon, so habits have time to form.
  3. Measure again with the same questions at the end, on the same team.
  4. Look at participation, not just satisfaction scores. A programme that people enjoyed but few attended has not reached your organisation.
  5. Act on what the numbers show, then measure again.

This is how we work at SoulCare™. Every programme is tracked through our Workplace Performance Index (WPI), so HR teams can show management what actually changed.

A better question for leaders: instead of asking "Are our employees aware of stress?", ask "What are we doing to help them manage it, week after week?"

Wellbeing as part of how you work

Sustainable wellbeing is not about teaching people to cope better with an unhealthy environment. It is about helping individuals and organisations build the awareness, skills and culture to work differently.

Lasting change takes time. When we invest that time, wellbeing stops being an event on the calendar and becomes part of how an organisation works.
